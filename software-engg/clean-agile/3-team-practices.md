# Extreme Programming (XP) Team Practices

## Overtime

Working overtime is not a demonstration of dedication to your employer.

More often, excessive overtime indicates:

* Poor planning
* Accepting unrealistic deadlines
* Making commitments that should not have been made
* Allowing yourself to be pressured rather than acting as a professional

This does not mean that all overtime is bad. Exceptional circumstances occasionally require extra effort. 
However, such situations should be rare, and teams should recognize that the long-term costs of overtime often 
exceed the short-term schedule gains.

Key Principle: Sustainable pace is more productive than heroic effort.

## Sleep

The most valuable resource in a programmer’s life is sufficient sleep.

Software development requires:

* Clear thinking
* Problem-solving
* Creativity
* Attention to detail

Lack of sleep reduces performance in all of these areas and increases the likelihood of mistakes.

Key Principle: Protect your sleep as carefully as you protect your development environment.


## Collective Ownership

Collective Ownership does not mean that specialization is forbidden.

As systems grow in complexity, specialization becomes necessary. No individual can fully understand every detail of a large system.

However, even specialists should regularly work outside their primary area of expertise.

Benefits of Collective Ownership

* Knowledge is shared across the team.
* Work does not stop when a particular developer is unavailable.
* Team members can help one another more effectively.
* The codebase remains a shared responsibility.

### Anti-Pattern: Module Ownership

Some teams operate under strict ownership rules where each developer “owns” a specific module and others avoid 
touching it.

This often leads to:

* Bottlenecks
* Finger-pointing
* Miscommunication
* Reduced collaboration
* Delayed progress when the owner is absent

Key Principle: Everyone owns the codebase; specialists simply have deeper expertise in certain areas.


## Stand-Up Meetings

Daily stand-ups are a common XP practice, but they are optional.

Guidelines:

* The meeting does not have to occur every day.
* Choose a frequency that fits the team’s needs.
* Keep it short (typically around 10 minutes).
* Focus on coordination rather than problem-solving.

Stand-Up Questions

Each team member answers three questions:

1. What did I accomplish since the last meeting?
2. What will I work on before the next meeting?
3. What obstacles are blocking my progress?

Any discussion that requires deeper investigation should be taken offline after the stand-up.

## The Chicken and the Pig

A pig and a chicken are walking down the road.

Chicken: “Hey Pig, I was thinking we should open a restaurant!”

Pig: “Maybe. What would we call it?”

Chicken: “How about Ham and Eggs?”

Pig: “No thanks. I’d be committed, but you’d only be involved.”

Lesson

The story illustrates the difference between:

* Committed participants — those doing the work and bearing the consequences.
* Interested participants — those who are involved but not directly accountable.

In the context of stand-up meetings:

* Developers are the primary participants and should speak.
* Managers, stakeholders, and observers may attend.
* Observers should avoid interrupting or directing the meeting.

Key Principle: Stand-ups are for the team to coordinate its work, not for management reporting.

Summary of XP Team Practices

* Maintain a sustainable pace; avoid routine overtime.
* Prioritize sufficient sleep.
* Encourage collective ownership of the codebase.
* Use stand-ups for quick team synchronization.
* Keep decision-making and communication focused on those committed to the work.
